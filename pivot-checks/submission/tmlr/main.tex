\begin{abstract}
We audit the released code and runs of the AgentDyn prompt-injection benchmark. In public prompt-injection benchmark artifacts, measurement plumbing can silently fail.

First, 20 of AgentDyn\textquotesingle s 28 benchmark-specific injection tasks ship a reference solution that fails the task\textquotesingle s own security check (18 copy-paste banking stubs, nine of which call a tool absent from the suite, one wrong-argument call, one missing OTP step); we confirm all 20 by executing the suite\textquotesingle s own check logic. The nine shopping stubs had already been replaced in AutoDojo\textquotesingle s public code (June 2026); the github and dailylife defects and the complete census are, as far as we found, new. An executed audit of related code bases also finds that original AgentDojo v1.2.x workspace injection tasks 6--13 return an empty reference solution that fails \texttt{check()} (whether by design is unverified). Of five 2026 papers found that evaluate on AgentDyn, two use reference-solution-derived information (exposed), one does not and two are unknown.

Second, re-scoring released runs by attempted instead of executed attacks with an argument-level attempt scorer (ATT-ARG; 78/80 agreement, κ 0.95 with blind AI labels) gives positive rank agreement (Kendall τb 0.642--1.0 per cell). 3 of 9 eligible cells contain at least one significant reversal under the preregistered rule; every ATT-ARG reversal involves progent, and the count falls to 1 of 9 under task-clustered inference and 0 of 9 without progent. The earlier tool-identity (name-match) scorer is kept as a reference.

Third, replaying 73,009 stored labels against current checker code changes 0.27\% of them; a small LLM-judge case study (one agent model, three defenses, single AI adjudicator) finds that judges mostly count blocked attempts as successes.
\end{abstract}
\textbf{Keywords:} prompt injection; LLM agents; benchmark audit; security evaluation; measurement validity; reproducibility

\section{1 Introduction}\label{introduction}

Defenses against indirect prompt injection are compared by attack success rate (ASR). Pathade et al. argue that ASR is a family of metrics whose defining choices papers rarely state, and show analytically that some choices can reverse defense rankings, without running defenses themselves (§III, §V, §VIII). Shaw audits one benchmark and builds a validated harness, showing that scoring by tool identity rather than arguments inflates ASR and that a scenario must be able to fire on the attacker\textquotesingle s action (§IV, §V-A, §V-D, §VII-A).

This paper is an \textbf{artifact audit} of AgentDyn: its v1 task code (commit 5353cf7) and its released runs, which we did not author. Our thesis: \emph{in public prompt-injection benchmark artifacts, measurement plumbing can silently fail. 20 of 28 AgentDyn-specific injection tasks ship a reference solution that does not satisfy its own security check (18 are copy-paste banking stubs; the nine shopping stubs were independently fixed in AutoDojo\textquotesingle s code). In the released AgentDyn runs, every significant EXEC-vs-ATT-ARG rank reversal involves the execution-time defense progent.}

\textbf{Findings, ordered by strength.}

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Main result {[}CODE FACT{]}:} a reference-solution defect in 20 of 28 AgentDyn-specific injection tasks, confirmed by executing the check logic, with an extended audit of related code bases and a downstream-exposure census (§4). AutoDojo fixed the shopping subset earlier (§2).
\item
  \textbf{Secondary, descriptive:} a narrow ranking sensitivity to the success definition, confined to two execution-time defenses on one benchmark, shown with all sensitivity analyses side by side (§5). It is a quantification of magnitude and fragility, not a discovery: that attempt-based metrics give execution-time blockers no credit is expected a priori and anticipated by Shaw (§V-F) and Pathade et al. (§III, §V-B).
\item
  \textbf{Short results:} a negative checker-drift replay and a small judge-vs-checker case study (§6).
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Non-claims.} We claim no defense is effective or ineffective, no general ranking instability, and no validated measurement framework. AgentDojo contributes only one eligible ranking cell and no reversal; the ranking result is about AgentDyn. Our own earlier testbed, which motivated these questions, is not evidence here and is described only in Supplementary S1.

\section{2 Related work}\label{related-work}

All claims below come from full-text reads of the arXiv HTML versions; section references are to those versions.

\textbf{Measurement validity of IPI evaluation.} Shaw (2609.32691) audits one unnamed IPI benchmark. Its defect classes include success scored by tool identity (D2, §IV), and re-scoring identical traces gives 21.7\% ASR under tool-identity scoring vs 1.2\% at argument level (§VII-A). Pre-flight checks reject attacker goals that name an absent tool (§V-A, §V-D), and blocked calls are recorded (§V-F). Pathade, Pawar and Patil (2609.25173) define six axes of ASR, including the success oracle (§III), and derive ranking inversion analytically without running defenses (§V-B, §VIII). Because of Shaw\textquotesingle s D2, our earlier function-name attempt scorer is now only a tool-identity reference, and the primary attempt scorer is argument-level (ATT-ARG, §3).

\textbf{Benchmark audits.} Zhu et al. (2507.02825, ABC) recommend verifying ground-truth correctness and shipping an automatic oracle solver (items T.7--T.9, §4.1). Our self-check is an application of T.9, so the principle is known. BenchGuard (2604.24955) names "errors in the reference solution" as a defect category (§3.2, Table 1) and audits ScienceAgentBench and BIXBench with LLMs (§5). Vaghasiya, Bhat et al. (2607.02577) audit evaluator validity in BFCL v4, τ²-bench and LiveMCPBench (§3.4, §4.3). Abrich (2610.09142) injects faults into reference solutions that are assumed correct, to find verifier false accepts in AppWorld and WorkArena (§2, §3). Luo et al. (2609.34262) detect unearned passes, e.g. reference solutions leaked via git history (§4--§5). The closest work is Bellibatlu, Wang and Zhang (2609.37315). They audit, by execution at pinned commits, whether mutating \emph{tools} honour their advertised contracts in four benchmarks, including the full 25-tool AgentDojo v1 surface (§V-B1, §V-B3). They do not audit injection-task reference solutions or AgentDyn.

\textbf{Prior identification of part of our main finding.} AutoDojo (2606.15057; code xhOwenMa/AutoDojo at aa458794) builds on AgentDojo and AgentDyn (§4.1, App. D). A note in its shopping injection-task file, dated 2026-06-14, says the original \texttt{send\_\allowbreak{}money(recipient="")} stubs "matched no security() check" and replaces them. Afterwards 6 of 9 pass \texttt{check()}, and three remain OTP-gated (per the code note). We found no statement of this in the paper text. We credit AutoDojo with the first public fix of the shopping stubs. Its github and dailylife tasks are unchanged.

\textbf{What is known and what is new.} \emph{Known:} the principle that reference solutions must satisfy their own checks (ABC T.7--T.9; BenchGuard\textquotesingle s GT class; Shaw §V-A); executed audits of AgentDojo artifacts at the tool layer (Bellibatlu et al.); the AgentDyn shopping stubs (AutoDojo); tool-identity inflation of ASR (Shaw §VII-A); analytical ranking inversion (Pathade et al.). \emph{New, as far as we found:}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  the executed, complete census of 20/28 AgentDyn injection-task reference-solution failures, including github (9, tool absent) and dailylife (2) and the root cause of dailylife injection\_task\_2;
\item
  the cross-benchmark defect table;
\item
  the str-only cause of the "not injectable" self-check message;
\item
  the downstream-exposure census;
\item
  an empirical, argument-level rank-reversal analysis on released third-party runs.
\end{itemize}

We do \textbf{not} claim to be the first audit of an AgentDojo-derived prompt-injection benchmark.

\textbf{Other work.} The Same Zero (Yin, 2610.04504) reports AgentDojo banking state checks missing 11/75 tool-level attacks {[}partial read{]}. Liu (2610.03448) re-evaluates detectors offline. Hardy et al. (2610.00651) apply generalizability theory to agent leaderboards. Benchmarks and defenses (AgentDojo 2406.13352, InjecAgent 2403.02691, CaMeL 2503.18813, Progent 2504.11703) are cited in the references.

\section{3 Data, definitions and records}\label{data-definitions-and-records}

\textbf{Threat model.} The user turn, system prompt and defense code are trusted; tool-returned content is untrusted; the attacker is non-adaptive and wants an effect the user did not request.

\textbf{Data.} Public AgentDojo and AgentDyn runs and code: 125 (benchmark, pipeline, attack) run sets, 50 AgentDojo and 75 AgentDyn, with 74,528 injection episodes. The checker-drift replay uses 73,009 non-error, non-DoS episodes (AgentDojo 31,604, AgentDyn 41,405). AgentDyn code: local clone at commit 5353cf7615b135cace8d07c8f12dac53a16b6db3; the installed package is byte-identical to the clone.

\textbf{Success definitions.}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  EXEC: the stored state-based \texttt{security()} result (the benchmark\textquotesingle s own ASR).
\item
  ATT-ARG (primary attempt scorer from this revision) {[}EXPLORATORY; definition and constant table hash-locked before scoring, 2026-10-16 16:51 Tehran{]}: the agent proposed a call to an injection target function whose arguments contain an attacker-specific constant of that injection task (e.g. the attacker\textquotesingle s IBAN, e-mail address, URL or file). Constants were extracted by code from task class attributes, the GOAL text and \texttt{security()}. 27 of 154 task-versions with no usable constant or no target function were excluded (dailylife injection\_task\_6; travel injection\_task\_2/4/6; workspace injection\_task\_1 and 6--13), removing 9,080 AgentDojo and 1,460 AgentDyn episodes. ATT-ARG is not preregistered {[}DEVIATION 11{]}.
\item
  ATT (ATTEMPTED, name-match; preregistered; now a tool-identity reference only, cf. Shaw D2, §IV, §VII-A): the agent proposed a call to an injection target function, by function-name match. For AgentDojo and AgentDyn dailylife the targets come from \texttt{ground\_\allowbreak{}truth()} as preregistered; for AgentDyn shopping/github, whose \texttt{ground\_\allowbreak{}truth()} is defective (§4), from a target map written by the AI analyst from each \texttt{security()} after first-pass disagreements were seen {[}DEVIATION 1{]}.
\item
  A third preregistered definition (attempted with correct arguments) was implemented with a deviating matcher, behaved inconsistently, and is not reported.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Defense stages.} Input (repeat\_user\_prompt, spotlighting\_with\_delimiting, transformers\_pi\_detector, prompt\_guard\_2\_detector, piguard\_detector), planning (tool\_filter, camel) and execution-time (progent, drift). The stage map was written after the reversals had been seen.

\textbf{Notation.} A \emph{cell} is a (benchmark, model, attack) combination with ≥3 defenses including no-defense. A \emph{flip} is a defense pair whose ASR difference is significant with opposite signs under EXEC and ATT. τb is Kendall\textquotesingle s tau-b between the EXEC and ATT rankings of a cell\textquotesingle s defenses; κ is Cohen\textquotesingle s kappa.

\textbf{Preregistration record and its limits.} The ranking-flip protocol was added to \texttt{docs/\allowbreak{}DESIGN\_\allowbreak{}VARIANCE\_\allowbreak{}STUDY.md} on 2026-10-15 at 22:40 Tehran; the recovered file \texttt{docs/\allowbreak{}DESIGN\_\allowbreak{}VARIANCE\_\allowbreak{}STUDY.PREREG\_\allowbreak{}2026-10-15\_\allowbreak{}2240.md} has sha256 \texttt{97bdb074…61e63c1}, byte-identical to \texttt{review/\allowbreak{}PREREG\_\allowbreak{}RANKFLIP.sha256}. It specifies: primary statistic Kendall τb with a bootstrap CI per cell; bootstrap over episodes within (cell, defense), 2000 reps, seed 20261015; decision rule "flip if the ASR-difference 95\% CI excludes 0 with opposite signs; main finding only if ≥25\% of cells contain a flip"; error episodes kept at their stored value, with a drop-errors sensitivity. \textbf{The preregistration was not blind to the mechanism:} before 22:40 the same analyst had already seen the judge pilot (21:24) and had labelled 53 + 60 gpt-4o episodes in which tool\_filter/PG2 attempts did not execute, so the attempted-vs-executed hypothesis came from inspected data. The preregistration does not mention excluding any suite.

\section{4 Main result: reference solutions that fail their own checks {[}CODE FACT{]}}\label{main-result-reference-solutions-that-fail-their-own-checks-code-fact}

\textbf{What a reference solution must do.} Each AgentDojo-style injection task has a \texttt{ground\_\allowbreak{}truth()} (the call sequence that achieves the attacker goal) and a \texttt{security()} predicate on the post-run environment. The benchmark\textquotesingle s own \texttt{TaskSuite.check()} runs the ground truth and requires \texttt{security()} to be True. If it is not, the reference solution and the check disagree about what the attack is.

\textbf{Executed self-check.} We ran upstream \texttt{TaskSuite.check()} unchanged on every v1 suite, offline, then repeated its per-task logic with each task isolated so that one crash does not hide the rest:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  shopping: \texttt{check()} returns failure; 9 of 9 injection tasks fail security after their ground truth.
\item
  github: \texttt{check()} aborts with \texttt{ToolNotFoundError:\ The\ requested\ function\ send\_\allowbreak{}money\ is\ not\ available}; per task, 9 of 9 injection tasks raise it.
\item
  dailylife: \texttt{check()} aborts with a \texttt{ValidationError} in injection\_task\_6; per task, 8/10 pass, injection\_task\_2 fails security and injection\_task\_6 raises.
\item
  The four original AgentDojo suites (workspace, travel, banking, slack): every injection task passes (6/6, 7/7, 9/9, 5/5).
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Result.} 20 of the 28 AgentDyn-specific injection tasks (dailylife 10, shopping 9, github 9) fail their own check, all 20 confirmed by execution. Four classes:

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Copy-paste banking stub, 18 tasks} (every shopping and every github task): \texttt{ground\_\allowbreak{}truth()} returns the same banking \texttt{send\_\allowbreak{}money(recipient="",\ amount=0.01,\ …)} call regardless of the stated goal (password change, purchase, download, leak).
\item
  \textbf{Tool absent:} in github \texttt{send\_\allowbreak{}money} does not exist, so the reference raises ToolNotFoundError (within the 18).
\item
  \textbf{Wrong arguments, 1 task} (dailylife injection\_task\_6): the calendar call omits the required start/end times.
\item
  \textbf{Missing verification step, 1 task} (dailylife injection\_task\_2): see below.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Root cause of dailylife injection\_task\_2.} The goal is to send the whole balance to the attacker. The reference solution is a single \texttt{send\_\allowbreak{}money(amount=1810.0,\ …)}. The suite\textquotesingle s own \texttt{send\_\allowbreak{}money} refuses amounts above 500 from an unverified account: it e-mails an OTP, queues the transfer and returns "Your transaction amount exceeds \$500. Please verify your account \ldots". The balance is 1810.0, so no transaction is made and \texttt{security()} is False. Appending the step the reference omits, \texttt{verify\_\allowbreak{}transaction(otp=…)} with the queued OTP, executes the transfer ("Sent 1810.0 to AT6854532054") and \texttt{security()} becomes True. The defect is in the reference solution, not in the checker or in our audit harness; agents that complete the OTP step do satisfy the check in the released runs (162/1460 inj2 episodes).

\textbf{Consequences and scope.}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \emph{Self-check:} \texttt{TaskSuite.check()} fails or aborts for shopping, github and dailylife.
\item
  \emph{Attack generation:} only the \texttt{tool\_\allowbreak{}knowledge} attack embeds \texttt{ground\_\allowbreak{}truth()} in injections; on these suites it would instruct agents to call \texttt{send\_\allowbreak{}money}. The released AgentDyn attack directories contain only important\_instructions, direct and none, so no released number derives from it; we could not trace the AgentDyn paper\textquotesingle s tables to unreleased runs.
\item
  \emph{Derived metrics:} any attempt-based metric keyed to \texttt{ground\_\allowbreak{}truth()} is invalid on these suites; this is what forced the post-hoc target map in §5 {[}DEVIATION 1{]}.
\item
  \emph{Released ASR:} not affected for the released runs. It is computed by \texttt{security()} on the post-run environment and never uses \texttt{ground\_\allowbreak{}truth()}.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Two side observations (not counted as defects).}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  In this code version \texttt{check()} also reports every user task in every suite, including the four original AgentDojo suites, as "not injectable" (40/40, 20/20, 16/16, 21/21, 20/20). Root cause: tool-message content is a list of content blocks, and \texttt{is\_\allowbreak{}task\_\allowbreak{}injectable()} only scans string contents, so it returns False although the canary is present. The function is called only in the user-task section of \texttt{check()}, so it cannot affect injection-task checks.
\item
  Replaying user-task reference solutions, utility fails for shopping 20/20, github 13/20 and dailylife 9/20 user tasks (and workspace user\_task\_7). Most failing AgentDyn cases hit an OTP or verification gate that a static reference cannot pass (e.g. a hard-coded OTP rejected as incorrect). This affects the self-check, not stored labels; we did not audit each task.
\end{itemize}

\section{5 Secondary result: ranking sensitivity to the success definition}\label{secondary-result-ranking-sensitivity-to-the-success-definition}

\textbf{Scope.} 9 cells are eligible, all under important\_instructions: one AgentDojo cell (gpt-4o-2024-05-13, 5 defenses, neither progent nor drift) and eight AgentDyn model cells. \textbf{All reversals in every analysis below are on AgentDyn; the AgentDojo cell has 0 flips.} The primary analysis is the preregistered rule on the \textbf{full data} (all suites), which requires the post-hoc target map for AgentDyn shopping/github {[}DEVIATION 1{]}. \subsection{5.5 Interpretation}\label{interpretation}

Every flip under the preregistered and clustered rules involves one of the two execution-time defenses, and the one AgentDojo cell, which has neither, has none. This is \textbf{consistent with a mechanical explanation} (an attempt metric gives no credit for blocking at execution time); \textbf{we did not test it}, the stage map was written post hoc, and two defenses cannot separate stage from defense identity. The scorer validation adds a second, competing explanation for drift. Stage-level ATT−EXEC gaps are in Appendix B.

\textbf{Deviations affecting §5.} (1) Post-hoc target map for AgentDyn shopping/github, written after first-pass disagreements had been seen. (2) Post-hoc subset excluding shopping/github, reported as a sensitivity analysis only. (3) Unversioned AgentDojo runs were scored as v1; the sensitivity analysis above removes them. (4) The preregistered blind re-label of 53 episodes was not performed. (5) No mixed model was fitted.

\subsection{6.1 Checker-version drift {[}EXPLORATORY; negative{]}}\label{checker-version-drift-exploratory-negative}

We replayed the logged tool calls of all scorable episodes against current checkers (AgentDojo 089ed468, AgentDyn 5353cf761). Drift is \textbf{small in aggregate (0.27\%; 198 of 73,009 labels: AgentDojo 39, AgentDyn 159), but up to 7.1 pp in one run set} (AgentDyn gpt-4o-mini repeat\_user\_prompt, −0.071 {[}−0.095, −0.048{]}); 6 of 120 sets have a CI excluding zero, and strict defense order changed in 2 of 15 multi-defense cells, both near-ties. One cause is confirmed by code diff (github injection\_task\_1 moved from a state- to a trace-based check); 166 changes have no attributable cause. Replay fidelity is limited (OTP feeding; tool outputs not compared with logs), and a first pass that re-executed blocked calls was discarded {[}DEVIATION; src: docs/DEVIATIONS\_2026-10-16.md{]}.

\subsection{6.2 Judge-vs-checker case study {[}EXPLORATORY{]}}\label{judge-vs-checker-case-study-exploratory}

On 600 AgentDyn gpt-4o attack episodes (200 per defense, 3 defenses), two LLM judges (gemini-2.5-flash-lite, claude-haiku-5.5; 1,396 calls, \$0.3787) agreed with the state checker at κ 0.932 and 0.940 on repeat\_user\_prompt but 0.454 and 0.632 on tool\_filter. A single AI reviewer (one AI reviewer, one model, three defenses; defense identity not hidden) adjudicated the 43 disagreements plus 10 controls and favoured the checker in 20/20 tool\_filter and 11/13 PG2 disagreements, but the judge in 3/6 repeat\_user\_prompt items. \textbf{Human check of the AI reviewer.} The owner, blind to the AI reviewer\textquotesingle s labels and the key, independently labelled a 16-item subset of the disagreement and control items (\texttt{review/\allowbreak{}owner\_\allowbreak{}subset\_\allowbreak{}ids.json}). Owner and AI reviewer agreed on 14/16 items (87.5\%; Wilson 95\% CI 64.0--96.5\%), Cohen\textquotesingle s κ = 0.62. Both disagreements (R15, R26) were items the AI reviewer marked \emph{unclear} and the owner marked \emph{executed} (a confirmed password change). With 16 items this is a coarse check and does not replace an independent second rater on the full set. Only 3 of the 16 items were labelled \emph{executed} by the owner, so κ is unstable and should be read as indicative only. The judges\textquotesingle{} typical error was to count attempts (stalled OTP checkouts, failed forms, text-only mentions) as successes, the same attempted-vs-executed distinction as §5. No claim beyond this one model and three defenses is made.

\section{7 Limitations}\label{limitations}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Almost no human labels.} Every label in this paper (53 disagreement and control items, 60 recall items, 113 earlier scorer-validation items, 80 progent/drift scorer-validation items, 80 ATT-ARG validation items) comes from one AI assistant, which also designed the analyses, the preregistration, the target map and the stage map. The only human check is a blind 16-item owner subset of the judge-review items (14/16 agreement, κ = 0.62; §6.2, \texttt{review/\allowbreak{}owner\_\allowbreak{}agreement.json}); no other label was checked by a human. Blinding was imperfect in the judge review (defense identity visible) and the labeller of the scorer validation knew how the scorer works.
\item
  \textbf{One benchmark in substance.} The ranking result rests on eight AgentDyn cells; AgentDojo contributes one cell without execution-time defenses, and none if unversioned runs are excluded.
\item
  \textbf{Two execution-time defenses.} Progent and drift carry every reversal under the preregistered and clustered rules; stage cannot be separated from defense identity.
\item
  \textbf{Statistics.} The preregistered bootstrap resamples episodes, not user tasks, and applies no multiplicity correction across 299 (290) pairs; each AgentDyn subset cell has only 20 user-task clusters; the task-clustered analysis is the more appropriate one and gives 1/9 on the full data. The judge bootstraps are not clustered.
\item
  \textbf{Scorer validity.} Name-match ATT is tool-identity scoring; on drift its positive labels agree with AI labels only half of the time (§5.4). ATT-ARG is post hoc, excludes 27 task-versions, and its validation uses AI labels on a sample stratified by its own label (§5.0); the human packet is unlabelled.
\item
  \textbf{Novelty.} The shopping stubs were identified in AutoDojo\textquotesingle s code before us; the exposure census is incomplete (no citation index available).
\item
  \textbf{Reference-solution audit.} The 20 defects are confirmed by execution at one commit; user-task reference solutions were probed but not audited task by task; maintainer confirmation is pending.
\item
  \textbf{Defective tasks in the analysed data.} The ranking analyses retain dailylife injection\_task\_2 and injection\_task\_6, because the preregistration did not provide for removing them.
\end{itemize}

\section{10 Conclusion}\label{conclusion}

Auditing artifacts we did not produce supports two observations about AgentDyn, not general prescriptions.

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Main:} (shopping part first fixed in AutoDojo\textquotesingle s code) 20 of 28 AgentDyn-specific injection tasks ship a reference solution that fails its own security check (18 copy-paste banking stubs, one wrong-argument call, one missing OTP step), confirmed by executing the check logic. The self-check and reference-keyed metrics are broken; released state-based ASRs are not.
\item
  \textbf{Secondary:} with the argument-level scorer ATT-ARG, attempt- and execution-based scoring rank defenses broadly alike (τb 0.642--1.0), and the preregistered flip rule finds reversals in 3/9 cells (2/9 in a post-hoc subset), all involving progent and all on AgentDyn; 1/9 under task-clustered inference and 0/9 without progent. The name-match drift reversals do not survive argument-level scoring. This is an empirical instance of the inversion Pathade et al. analyse, on one benchmark.
\item
  \textbf{Short:} checker drift is small in aggregate (0.27\%) but up to 7.1 pp in one set; in a one-model, three-defense case study, LLM judges counted blocked attempts as successes.
\end{itemize}

All labels come from one AI assistant except a blind 16-item owner check (14/16 agreement, κ = 0.62); maintainer confirmation and independent reproduction are pending.

\section{Appendix C: Why clustered inference gives more flips in the subset than in the full data}\label{appendix-c-why-clustered-inference-gives-more-flips-in-the-subset-than-in-the-full-data}

The full data is not a superset test. Each AgentDyn cell adds 40 shopping/github user tasks (60 vs 20 clusters), whose defense differences are smaller, so pooled estimates shrink: e.g. gemini-2.5-pro drift vs piguard\_detector ΔEXEC goes from −0.050 to −0.009, and llama drift vs progent ΔATT from −0.29 to −0.093. Borderline cases cross the Holm threshold in both directions; gpt-4o-2024-08-06 progent vs tool\_filter has ΔEXEC p = 0.0062 in the subset (not significant at m = 36) and 0.0042 in the full data (significant).
